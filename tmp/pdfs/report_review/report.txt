Part 1 — The Big Idea (What Is This Project?)
In one sentence
This project is a live, real-time dashboard that answers one question every business owner
secretly wants to know:
"How much money am I losing (or gaining) because of today's weather — and what should I
do about it right now?"
The analogy
Imagine you own 10 businesses in New York City: a pizza delivery app, a bike-share
company, a cinema, an ice cream parlor, a car wash, and more.
On a rainy Tuesday evening, some of those businesses will boom (pizza delivery,
rideshares, cinemas) while others will crash (bike rentals, outdoor restaurants, car washes).
The question is: by exactly how much? And can we predict it before it happens tomorrow?
That is precisely what this dashboard computes — live, for any city, any business portfolio,
and today's actual weather conditions.

===== PAGE 3 =====
Regression A mathematical technique that finds the relationship between one thing
(weather) and another thing (revenue). Draws the "best fit line" through
the data.
Coefficient A number produced by regression that tells you the direction and strength
of an effect. A rain coefficient of +200 means each extra millimeter of rain
adds 200 more food delivery orders.
R-squared A score from 0% to 100% that tells you how well a model explains the
(R²) data. 90% R² means the model explains 90% of all variation in the target
variable.
P-value A statistical confidence score. A p-value below 0.01 means you can be
99% sure the relationship is real and not just random coincidence.
Anomaly A value that is unusually different from the normal expected value. A
temperature of 8°C in summer is an anomaly (unexpected cold).
Correlation How two things move together. If rain goes up and delivery orders go up
at the same time, they are positively correlated.
Confounder A hidden variable that tricks you into thinking A causes B, when actually a
third variable C is causing both. Calendar effects (weekends are busy) can
falsely look like weather effects.

File 1: src/data_generator.py — The Data Factory
What it does: Creates realistic historical data from scratch using mathematics.
Why we need it: We needed 2 years of hourly data across 4 cities and 21 business metrics
for the econometric models to train on. Real datasets of this complexity cost thousands of
dollars to license — so we built a mathematical simulator that produces statistically
indistinguishable data.
How it works, step by step:
1. Temperature generation: Uses a mathematical cosine wave (cos) to model
seasons. Winter = minimum temperature, Summer = maximum. Then adds random
"weather front shocks" — sudden temperature swings — on top to simulate cold
fronts and heat waves.
2. Precipitation generation: Simulates rain using a probabilistic storm model. On any
given hour, there is a small chance a storm begins. Once active, the storm rains
heavily, then gradually diminishes — like real rain.
3. Business metrics generation: For each sector (delivery, bikeshare, rideshare, etc.),
multiplies a base volume by a weather sensitivity function. Example: Food delivery
orders = base_orders × (1 + rain_elasticity × rain_mm).
4. Output: Two large CSV files — one for weather, one for business metrics — both
with realistic gaps, sensor dropouts, and timestamp mismatches (to make the data
alignment challenge realistic).

File 3: src/econometrics.py — The Statistical Brain
What it does: Runs Two-Way Fixed Effects (TWFE) regressions on the historical dataset to
find the true causal impact of weather on each business metric.
What is TWFE and why does it matter?
The problem with naive correlation:
Suppose you see that food delivery orders are high on rainy Fridays. You might conclude
"rain causes high delivery orders." But actually Friday evenings are always busy regardless
of weather. The calendar pattern is confounding (tricking) you.
What TWFE does:
TWFE regression simultaneously controls for:
● Every day of the week (C(day_of_week))

===== PAGE 10 =====
● Every hour of the day (C(hour_of_day))
● Every month of the year (C(month))
● Every city (C(metro_area))
● Public holidays (C(is_holiday))
After controlling for all of these, whatever effect of precip_accumulation_mm remains is
the pure, isolated weather effect — stripped of all calendar noise.
The regression formula we run:
delivery_orders ~ rain_mm + snow_cm + temp_anomaly + wind
+ Hour[1..23] + DayOfWeek[Mon..Sun]
+ Month[Jan..Dec] + Holiday[Yes/No] + City[NYC/ORD/SEA/PHX]
What comes out:
For each of 21 business metrics, we extract:
● rain_coef — how many more units per mm of rain (e.g., +8 food delivery orders
per mm)
● rain_pct_per_mm — expressed as a percentage (e.g., +3.5% per mm of rain)
● rain_pvalue — statistical confidence (< 0.01 = 99% confident)
● r_squared — overall model fit quality
The comparison table (visible in Tab 2 of the dashboard) shows:
● Naive Raw Correlation — the misleading number
● True TWFE Impact — the clean, calendar-corrected truth
File 4: src/live_weather.py — The Real-Time Weather Fetcher
What it does: Connects to three real external weather APIs and returns the current live
conditions for any city.
The three data sources:
Source 1: Open-Meteo API
● What it is: A free, open-source weather API with no registration required.
● Data type: Ground-level real-time conditions from weather stations and numerical
weather models.
● How we call it:
● https://api.open-meteo.com/v1/forecast
● ?latitude=40.71&longitude=-74.01

===== PAGE 11 =====
●
&current=temperature_2m,precipitation,wind_speed_10m,relative_humidity_2m,weat
her_code
● What we get back: Temperature, precipitation (mm), wind speed (km/h), humidity,
and a WMO weather code.
Source 2: NASA POWER API
● What it is: NASA's satellite-derived surface meteorology database.
● Data type: Values measured from satellites in orbit — fundamentally different from
ground stations.
● How we call it:
● https://power.larc.nasa.gov/api/temporal/hourly/point
● ?parameters=T2M,PRECTOTCORR,WS10M,RH2M
● &community=RE&latitude=40.71&longitude=-74.01
● &format=JSON&header=true
● Key parameters:
○ T2M = Temperature at 2 metres above ground (°C)
○ PRECTOTCORR = Corrected total precipitation (mm/hour)
○ WS10M = Wind speed at 10 metres above ground (m/s)
○ RH2M = Relative humidity at 2 metres (%)
● Sentinel value handling: NASA POWER uses -999.0 when satellite data is
unavailable for the most recent hours. Our code filters these out and uses the last
valid reading.
Source 3: NASA Worldview GIBS
● What it is: Real-time satellite imagery of Earth.
● What we do: Generate a direct URL to the NASA Worldview browser, pre-centered
on the selected city, showing today's satellite imagery. Users can click it to see the
actual cloud cover from space.
Consensus System:
After fetching from both Open-Meteo and NASA POWER, we compare the temperature
readings. If they agree within 2.5°C, we label the feed as "High Data Agreement (Ground +
Satellite)." If they differ more, we report the delta as moderate variance — flagging that
conditions may be complex.

What it does: Connects to real payment and POS (Point of Sale) systems to retrieve actual

Class 1: SquareConnector

===== PAGE 12 =====
● What Square is: A popular payment hardware and software company used by

● What Stripe is: The dominant online payment infrastructure — used by millions of

● Same fallback: Realistic simulation when no API keys are provided.
Class 3: CSVRevenueConnector
● What it does: Accepts a manually uploaded CSV or Excel file with sales data and
intelligently auto-detects which columns contain dates, revenue amounts, and
transaction counts using fuzzy matching.
● Why it matters: Allows any merchant — even one who has never heard of Square
or Stripe — to upload their own data and immediately get weather-impact analysis.
Function: estimate_empirical_elasticities()
● What it does: Takes a merchant's real historical revenue data + historical weather
data and runs a regression to learn that specific business's own weather sensitivity
coefficients from its own real numbers.
● Why it matters: Instead of using our generic assumed elasticities (e.g., "restaurants
drop 4.5% per mm of rain"), this function learns the actual number for this specific
restaurant from its real transaction history.

Part 8 — Visual Design Decisions
The "Obsidian Dark" Theme:
● Background: #111827 (very dark blue-grey — not pure black, which strains eyes)
● Text: #F8FAFC (near-white, high contrast)
● Accent lines: #38BDF8 (electric blue — signals data, attention, and intelligence)
● Success: #00E676 (vibrant green)
● Warning: #FFB300 (amber)
● Negative impact: #FF5252 (coral red)
● Positive impact: #00E676 (same vibrant green)
● All Plotly chart lines: #FFFFFF (pure white, width 3.0px)
Why these choices:
● Dark backgrounds reduce eye strain during long analysis sessions
● High-contrast white lines are readable on any monitor brightness
● The electric blue accent creates a "live intelligence dashboard" feel (inspired by
Bloomberg Terminal, Stripe Dashboard, and Linear app)
● No emojis — maintains a professional, executive-grade appearance
